A small network, opened up

Inside a small CNN

This network looks at a 32×32 photo and picks one of ten things it might show. It has learned numbers, and it labels of photos it never saw in training correctly.

Everything below is the real trained model. Your browser runs the forward pass on the actual weights, so when you change the photo, every figure on this page changes with it. Pick a photo to carry through the story. The rail keeps it in view.

The whole network

Six steps from pixels to a guess

The photo passes through three convolution blocks, which turn colours into patterns, and pooling, which trades position for reach. A single linear layer then scores each class. These thumbnails show the strongest channel at each stage, for your photo. Select a stage to jump to it.

The input

A photo is three grids of numbers

The network never sees a picture. It gets 32×32×3 numbers: one grid each for red, green and blue. Before anything else, each channel is shifted and scaled so that across the training set it has mean 0 and standard deviation 1. Hover a pixel to read it.

One operation

A convolution is a small template, slid everywhere

A filter is a 3×3 grid of weights. At each position it multiplies the numbers under it by its weights and adds them up. The result is high wherever the patch matches the template. This demo uses one grey channel to keep the arithmetic readable. Change a weight, or try a preset.

Blue is negative and red is positive. The output grid is the same size as the input, so a filter's response keeps its position. Real layers apply many filters at once, each producing its own output grid, called a channel.

The first layer

Nobody designed these filters

These are the 32 filters of the first layer, drawn as tiny colour patches. They began as random noise, and training shaped them. At 3×3 they are hard to read as pictures, so judge them by what they do: select a filter to see where it fires on your photo. Then move through the epochs in the rail, and the filters, the map and the prediction all change together.

Layer by layer

What each step does to your photo

Step through the stages. A convolution makes many channels, positive and negative. ReLU zeroes every negative value, so a channel only reports what it found. Pooling keeps the largest of each 2×2 block: the map halves in size, and a feature that moves by a pixel usually still lands in the same block.

Channels are sorted by total activity. Dark means a strong response, and all tiles at a stage share one scale, except where a stage has negative values, shown in blue and red.

Reach

Later units see more of the photo

A unit in the first layer sees 3 pixels across. Each later step widens that: 4, 8, 10 and finally 18 pixels. That is why later channels can respond to shapes and not only edges. Hover the photo to place a unit. The outline shows every pixel that can influence it.

which pixels actually matter, averaged over 256 test photos

The heat map is the effective receptive field: the gradient of a central unit with respect to the input. It is concentrated towards the middle of the outline. A unit can see the whole box, but the centre has the most influence.

What a channel looks for

Two ways to ask a channel what it wants

The first answer is empirical: search 10,000 test photos for the crops that excite the channel most. The second is synthetic: start from noise and adjust the pixels to excite the channel as much as possible. Real crops show what it fires on in practice, and they are the stronger evidence. Synthetic images can look like more than they are, so read them alongside the crops.

The decision

Where the evidence came from

The last layer is linear: each of the 4,096 values gets one weight per class, and a class's score is their weighted sum. So the score splits exactly across the 8×8 positions. Moving the photo

Does a CNN care where things are?

Convolutions treat every position alike, so you might expect a CNN to ignore where an object sits. But this network's last layer reads the 8×8 grid position by position. Slide the photo and watch the confidence, then compare with the measured accuracy over the whole test set.
