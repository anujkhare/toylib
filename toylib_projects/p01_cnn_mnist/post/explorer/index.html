A trained network, opened up

Why does a CNN work?

This network labels of photos it never saw in training, with one of ten classes. A model that treats pixels as a plain list gets 58%. This page follows one photo through the network to see why.

Every number is from the real model. The views below are saved results for these photos. Upload your own, or cover part of one, and your browser runs the whole network on the real weights. Pick a photo. The rail keeps it in view.

Before convolution

Fewer weights, better answers

All four models trained for 200 epochs with the same augmentation and optimizer. The MLP spends 19.7 million weights on patterns tied to one place in the frame. The small CNN uses less than 2% of that and beats it by 25 points, because each filter is reused at every position.

One operation

A convolution is a small template, slid everywhere

At each position, a filter multiplies the numbers under it by its weights and adds them up. The result is high wherever the patch matches the template. This demo uses one grey channel of your photo. Change a weight or try a preset.

Blue is negative and red is positive. The output keeps the layout of the input, so the response to a feature stays where the feature is. That grid of responses is a feature map.

Layer by layer

What the layers see

Early maps still look like the photo, because they respond to edges and colours at each place. The maps shrink from 80×80 to 10×10 on the way through, and they become coarse blobs that say what is present and only roughly where.

The strongest channels on this photo, sorted by mean activity. Dark means a strong response.

Reach

Later units can use more of the photo

Each layer adds to how far a unit can reach: 7 pixels across after the first convolution, 219 after the last, which is more than the whole photo. That is why late maps can respond to objects. The heat maps show how much a central unit actually depends on each pixel, and most of it stays near the middle of that reach.

The gradient of one central unit, averaged over 32 test photos, on a square-root scale. It never quite reaches zero far away, because GroupNorm's statistics cover the whole image. The numbers give reach through convolutions.

Why 18 layers can train

Normalization and skips fix different things

These curves come from untrained 56-layer networks, so they show what the architecture does before learning starts. Without either tool, activations fade by about ten orders of magnitude by the last block. GroupNorm holds them steady, but gradients grow about 50 times on the way back to the first block. Skips alone keep gradients level but let activations grow. With both, each stays within a factor of about six.

One batch of 64 photos, resized to 32×32, on one random initialization. Block 0 is nearest the input.
